\section{Key Changes}

This section lists key changes made to the RaM process since my dissertation. 

\subsection{Equilibration Step}\label{ss:equilibration}

This change adds a step to the restart of the RaM workflow. The dissertation restarts the simulation directly in dynamics from the remapped fields, with the velocity $\mathbf{v}$ and acceleration $\mathbf{a}$ ``remapped and restarted normally''. A short equilibration step is now taken first. It brings the remapped state back into the dynamic equilibrium it satisfied before the remap, without recording the correction as motion.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Remapping to a new mesh leaves the restarted state out of discrete equilibrium. Each field is transferred accurately on its own, but the internal forces assembled from the remapped stress on the new mesh no longer balance the remapped inertia and the boundary loads. 

The first time step removes the imbalance by moving the mesh. The Newmark update reads this correction as motion:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{a}_{n+1} = \frac{1}{\beta\Delta t^2}\left(\mathbf{u}_{n+1}-\mathbf{u}_n\right) - \frac{1}{\beta\Delta t}\mathbf{v}_n - \left(\frac{1}{2\beta}-1\right)\mathbf{a}_n.\label{eq:newmark_accel}
\end{equation}
A fixed correction $\Delta\mathbf{u}$ therefore produces a spurious acceleration of order $\Delta\mathbf{u}/(\beta\Delta t^2)$, which grows as the time step shrinks. A time-step cut after a difficult restart therefore makes the artifact worse rather than better. In TET10 meshes the artifact concentrates on the mid-edge nodes inside the damage band. 

\paragraph{Implementation.}
Before the remap, the state at step $n$ satisfies the HHT-$\alpha$ balance of momentum,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}_n + (1+\alpha)\,\mathbf{f}^\text{int}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_n) - \alpha\,\mathbf{f}^\text{int}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n-1}) = \mathbf{f}^\text{ext},\label{eq:hht_balance}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{f}^\text{int}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) = \int \boldsymbol{\sigma} : \nabla\mathbf{w}\,dV$ is the internal force of a stress field. The restart now begins with an equilibration step of negligible length ($\Delta t_\text{eq}=10^{-6}$) that solves the same balance on the new mesh for the displacement $\mathbf{u}$, with the inertia and the old stress taken from the remapped fields:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}_\text{rec} + (1+\alpha)\,\mathbf{f}^\text{int}\big(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})\big) - \alpha\,\mathbf{f}^\text{int}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n-1,\text{rec}}) = \mathbf{f}^\text{ext}.\label{eq:equilibration}
\end{equation}
If the remap introduced no error, $\mathbf{u}$ does not move and the restart continues exactly as before. Otherwise the step absorbs the remap error into the configuration. One such step is normally enough, after which the time step returns to its dynamic value. During the equilibration window:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The inertial force is \emph{frozen} at the remapped acceleration, $\rho\,\mathbf{a}_\text{rec}$, in place of the Newmark inertia term, so the step keeps the inertial force the body carried before the remap.
    \item The HHT-$\alpha$ terms are carried. The old stress in Eq.~\eqref{eq:equilibration} is the stress of the step before the remap, $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n-1}$, which is remapped along with the stress. 
    \item The Newmark velocity and acceleration updates are suspended, so the equilibrating displacement is not recorded as motion. The remapped $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ therefore reach the first dynamic step unchanged.
    \item The coupling between the mechanics and the damage problem is held: the damage problem keeps its previous driving energy, and its damage is not passed back to the mechanics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Projecting $\Ubar$ instead of $\U$}\label{ss:ubar}

This change concerns the restart of the F-bar corrected deformation gradient . The dissertation remaps the stretch $\U$ of the uncorrected deformation gradient and reapplies the F-bar correction on the new mesh. The corrected stretch $\Ubar$ is now remapped instead, and the correction is not reapplied.

\paragraph{Dissertation approach.}
The F-bar method replaces the volume change at each quadrature point with its element average,
\begin{equation}
    \Fbar = \left(\frac{\bar{J}}{J}\right)^{1/3}\F,\qquad \bar{J} = \frac{1}{V_0}\int_{V_0} J\,dV_0,
\end{equation}
where $J=\det\F$ and the average is taken over the element in the reference configuration of the simulation. In the dissertation, the polar decomposition $\F=\mathbf{R}\U$ of the uncorrected deformation gradient is written out, $\mathbf{R}$ and $\U$ are remapped, and on restart the correction is rebuilt from $\F_{0\rightarrow1}=\mathbf{R}\U$ by averaging $\det\F_{0\rightarrow1}$ over the element of the new mesh.
%
%The new mesh is the deformed configuration of the previous step, so this second average is taken over the deformed volume rather than the reference volume. Even on the same mesh, without any remapping, the two averages differ: with $dV_0 = J\,dV_0$,
%\begin{equation}
%    \frac{1}{V_1}\int_{V_1} J\,dV_1 = \frac{\int_{V_0} J^2\,dV_0}{\int_{V_0} J\,dV_0} = \bar{J} + \frac{1}{\bar{J}}\,\frac{1}{V_0}\int_{V_0}\left(J-\bar{J}\right)^2 dV_0 \;\geq\; \bar{J}.
%\end{equation}
%Reapplying the correction therefore inflates the averaged volume change by the variance of $J$ within the element, and it is exact only where $J$ is uniform. That is precisely where the correction does no work: the elements in which F-bar matters, nearly incompressible or plastically flowing, are the ones in which $J$ varies within the element. On a different mesh the averaging region also changes, so the restarted $\Fbar$ depends on the new elements rather than on the material state.
%
\paragraph{New approach.}
Because the F-bar correction scales $\F$ by a scalar, $\Fbar$ has the same rotation as $\F$:
\begin{equation}
    \Fbar = \mathbf{R}\Ubar,\qquad \Ubar = \mathbf{R}^T\Fbar = \left(\frac{\bar{J}}{J}\right)^{1/3}\U.
\end{equation}
The corrected stretch $\Ubar$ is symmetric positive definite with $\det\Ubar=\bar{J}$, so it belongs to the same space as $\U$ and is remapped with the same Lie-group interpolation (its logarithm is interpolated and mapped back). On restart the corrected deformation gradient is taken directly from the remapped fields,
\begin{equation}
    \Fbar_{0\rightarrow1} = \mathbf{R}\,\Ubar,
\end{equation}
with no averaging on the new mesh, and the total corrected deformation gradient is accumulated as before, $\Fbar = \Fbar_{0\rightarrow1}\,\Fbar_1$, where $\Fbar_1$ is the F-bar corrected deformation gradient of the new simulation step.

The volume change carried across the remap is then the element average computed in the simulation that produced it. A restart on the same mesh, without remapping, reproduces the corrected deformation gradient exactly, and after remapping the only error left is that of the interpolation itself.

\subsection{Irreversibility enforcement for TET10 elements}\label{ss:bernstein}

This change concerns how the damage irreversibility constraint $\dot{d}\geq0$ is enforced on TET10 meshes. The phase field is now discretized with quadratic Bernstein shape functions instead of quadratic Lagrange shape functions, which lets the constraint be enforced exactly by a variational inequality (VI) solver. Previously, a penalty method was used on TET10 meshes.

\paragraph{Bounds on the degrees of freedom.}
A VI solver enforces bounds on the degrees of freedom of the discrete field, here
\begin{equation}
    d_{a,n-1} \leq d_{a,n} \leq 1
\end{equation}
for every degree of freedom $a$, where $n$ is the time step. The constraint that matters, however, is on the field $d(\mathbf{X}) = \sum_a N_a(\mathbf{X})\,d_a$ itself. Bounds on the degrees of freedom imply bounds on the field only if the shape functions are non-negative and sum to one, so that the field is a weighted average of its degrees of freedom. Linear Lagrange shape functions have this property; quadratic Lagrange shape functions do not.

\paragraph{Quadratic Lagrange.}
Along an element edge with coordinate $s\in[0,1]$, the quadratic Lagrange shape functions of the two end nodes, $(1-s)(1-2s)$ and $s(2s-1)$, are negative over half of the edge (Figure~\ref{fig:shape_lagrange}). Bounding the nodal values therefore does not bound the field between the nodes: with $d=1$ at the mid-edge node and lower values at the end nodes, the field exceeds one between them. The VI problem posed on these degrees of freedom is also poorly behaved. The field oscillated at the mid-edge nodes. This is why a penalty method was used on TET10 meshes, which enforces irreversibility only approximately and needs a penalty parameter.

\paragraph{Quadratic Bernstein.}
Quadratic Bernstein shape functions span the same quadratic space, so the discretization is equally accurate, but every shape function is non-negative. Irreversibility and the bounds on $d$ hold pointwise, not only at the nodes, and the VI solver converges. \input{methodology/figures/shape_functions.tex}


\subsection{Change to Neo-Hookean Formulation}\label{ss:cnh_change}

This change replaces the volumetric part of the compressible Neo-Hookean (CNH) energy. The isochoric part is unchanged. This suggestion and formulation came from Jay.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Elements that are both heavily damaged and strongly compressed are the ones that invert at RaM restarts, when the remapped state is brought back into equilibrium on the new mesh. A standard CNH volumetric energy offers only a bounded resistance to collapse. Its Kirchhoff pressure tends to the finite value $-K/2$ as $J\to0$, so a compressive load that exceeds it can drive an element to zero volume, and the solve then fails with a negative Jacobian.

\paragraph{Formulation.}
Both models share the same isochoric energy,
\begin{equation}
    W(\bar{\mathbf{C}}) = \frac{1}{2}G\left(\operatorname{tr}\bar{\mathbf{C}}-3\right),\qquad \bar{\mathbf{C}} = J^{-2/3}\mathbf{C},
\end{equation}
written here without plasticity. They differ in the volumetric energy $U(J)$ and therefore in the volumetric Kirchhoff pressure $p = J\,U'(J)$, the volumetric stress being $p\,\mathbf{I}$. The dissertation uses
\begin{equation}
    U_\text{D}(J) = \frac{1}{2}K\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(J^2-1\right)-\ln J\right],\qquad p_\text{D}(J) = \frac{1}{2}K\left(J^2-1\right).\label{eq:cnh_vol_borden}
\end{equation}
The modified model uses the Seth---Hill volumetric energy of order $m=1$ (Garanger et al., Eq.~4),
\begin{equation}
    U_\text{M}(J) = \frac{1}{4}K\left[\left(J-1\right)^2+\left(J^{-1}-1\right)^2\right],\qquad p_\text{M}(J) = \frac{1}{2}K\left(J-J^{-1}\right)\left(J+J^{-1}-1\right).\label{eq:cnh_vol_jay}
\end{equation}
The deviatoric part of the Seth---Hill model in the same reference is not adopted.

\paragraph{Comparison.}
Figure~\ref{fig:cnh_potentials} compares the two volumetric responses.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small strain.} Both energies vanish at $J=1$ and have $U''(1)=K$, so the two models have the same bulk modulus and agree for small volume changes.
    \item \textbf{Tension.} For $J\to\infty$ both grow as $U\sim KJ^2/4$ and $p\sim KJ^2/2$, so their responses to large expansion are similar.
    \item \textbf{Compression.} Here they differ. As $J\to0$, $U_\text{D}$ grows only logarithmically and $p_\text{D}\to-K/2$, a bounded pressure. $U_\text{M}$ grows as $K/(4J^2)$ and $p_\text{M}\sim-K/(2J^2)$, which diverges. The modified model therefore resists collapse with a pressure that grows without bound as the element approaches inversion.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Custom Jacobian Damper}\label{ss:damper}

This change adds a damper to the Newton solve of the mechanics, used on RaM restarts. It limits each Newton step so that no element of the deformed mesh inverts.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
The first Newton iterations of a restart equilibrate the remapped state, which starts out of balance (Section~\ref{ss:equilibration}). On meshes with strongly distorted elements, such as the thin, curved TET10 elements along a damage band, a full Newton step can fold an element: the determinant of its element map becomes negative at a quadrature point, and the residual can no longer be evaluated. This happens however small the time step is, because the step that folds the element is the equilibration of the remapped state and not the load increment. A damper that evaluates each element at the trial configuration cannot handle this case: on a folded element it fails instead of shortening the step.

\textbf{Details are specific to MOOSE, so I will not write everything out here. It boils down to creating a damper that predicts inversion rather than reacting to large Jacobian changes post solution update.}
